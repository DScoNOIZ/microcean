Architecture: five layers of assembly

The system is divided into layers by the degree of "intelligence" of the solutions. Heavy universal models are brought in only where there is no way around them; the main work is done by lightweight specialized components.

LayerResponsibilityMechanisms
Coordination (top) Accepting a task in natural language; splitting it into sub-tasks; resolving conflicts between fragments; overall coordination of the assembly. A high-level model; invoked pointwise, not for every operation.
Swarm of micro-networks (middle) Verification of fragment contracts, generation of interfaces, fitness assessment. Many small specialized networks; activation by task type (sparse MoE — "sparse mixture of experts": only the network fitting the task is active).
Evolutionary selection Choosing combinations of fragments: mutation, crossover, selection by the fitness function. GA (genetic algorithm) / GP (genetic programming) over subsets of fragments; fitness ("adaptedness") = error + weight + conflicts; Holland's schema theorem as the theoretical foundation.
P2P infrastructure Search for fragments, propagation of updates, state synchronization without a central server. Kademlia DHT (distributed hash table: a shared "address book" of the network without a main server) — lookup in O(log n); push-pull gossip (neighbors pass news along like rumors) — dissemination in O(log n); CRDT (data structures that converge to a common form on their own) — replica convergence; rarest-first — replication of rare fragments.
Trust Consensus and reputation without energy-hungry computation. PBFT (consensus for the case of lying participants, 3f+1) for the council of coordinators; Raft (consensus for honest participants, 2f+1) inside a trusted cluster; hashgraph aBFT as the DAG variant; reputation = executable tests + Sybil protection (protection against a crowd of fake identities, Douceur, 2002).

The working cycle of an assembly

  1. Task statement. The user or an agent formulates the requirement at a high level: "assemble a document search engine with a vector index".
  2. Decomposition. The planner splits the requirement into classes of sub-tasks: parsing, storage, search, network protocol, encryption.
  3. Search for candidates. DHT queries find fragments of each class: tokenizers, indexes, TLS wrappers, authenticated encryption (AEAD).
  4. Evolutionary matching. The genetic algorithm goes over combinations, rejects incompatible interfaces, develops viable variants.
  5. Verification by the swarm. The micro-networks check every fragment against the contracts; non-conforming ones are discarded.
  6. Conflict resolution. The top level settles disputes between fragments and repairs integrations.
  7. Final assembly and testing. A full run of the test suite on the assembled program.
  8. Return to the network. A successful assembly is published back as a fragment — the ecosystem grows.

The road to a prototype

The first practical embryo already exists in miniature: the needed functions are manually "pulled" out of modules instead of installing a package whole (a "graph of skills" — skill-graph — and the resource-frugal practice of the publication's author). To automate such extraction is the goal of the project: make it automatic and decentralized — and we get a working minimal prototype of Microcean. The stage estimates are the author's rough guess, not a promise of deadlines: the fragment registry and search — weeks of work; evolutionary assembly for one language — the next quarter; the verifier swarm — a parallel branch.
