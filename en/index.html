Microcean is a concept of a planetary software ecosystem in which a program is neither written by hand nor assembled from large ready-made modules but — by the design of the concept — is assembled by a network automatically, from microscopic code fragments selected for a specific task.

This page is the starting point. It gives a brief and uniform description of the whole concept. Each section ends with links to the detailed materials of three levels of depth: for the general audience, for technical specialists, and for scientists. Choose your level — and move on.

1.The essence of the concept

Picture a single environment in which all the software of the planet lives: all the code, its whole history, all development practices. Today these things are scattered across separate tools — repositories, version-control systems, library marketplaces. In Microcean they are united into one interconnected system, figuratively called the Ocean of Code.

The user states a task in ordinary words — the way one explains to a handyman what needs fixing. Examples from the simplest to the professional ones:

  • "I want a game where a rocket flies around and collects stars" — a schoolchild's wording;
  • "I need a program that reminds Mom to take her medicine and tells me if she forgot" — caring for loved ones;
  • "Assemble a website for my flower workshop: a storefront, orders, card payments" — small business;
  • "Assemble a warehouse-management system with receiving, inventory and reports" — specialist level.

The system itself finds suitable code fragments on the network, checks their compatibility, joins them, tests them and delivers a finished program. From millions of fragments one ocean is made; from a billion drops — one water.

In the future a program is not written by hand and not assembled from huge modules — it is assembled by a network from microscopic fragments under a single high-level task. A rough analogy: the way a torrent file is assembled from pieces downloaded from thousands of computers at once.

2.What problem it solves

Modern development is built on large ready-made packages: to use a few functions, a project pulls in a whole library — often together with hundreds of kilobytes of unused code, dependencies and potential vulnerabilities. Such a monoculture makes the industry fragile: an error or malicious code in one popular library affects millions of systems.

IncidentYearConsequence
left-pad — removal of an 11-line package2016a significant part of the npm ecosystem stopped
SolarWinds — compromise of the update channel2020about 18 thousand organizations downloaded the compromised update; hundreds were actually breached
Log4Shell (CVE-2021-44228)2021a vulnerability in a single library — hundreds of millions of systems
xz utils — an implanted backdoor (CVE-2024-3094)2024a years-long targeted attack on open-source infrastructure

Besides, today's tools were designed for a human working by hand, not for a machine. The artificial intelligence that increasingly takes part in development needs an environment of a different order — one where searching, checking and joining code happen by themselves.

And the third trouble — irresponsibility toward resources. Installing one program from source can quietly download many gigabytes of dependencies: without warning and without consent — and a report of what exactly was downloaded, as a rule, never reaches the ordinary user. Optimization happens only under the pressure of a crisis — when a program stops working even on the most powerful hardware, it is squeezed somehow, and that is counted an achievement. Correctness tests exist almost everywhere; performance and load tests are, with rare exceptions (Firefox, Chromium, where such checks run on practically every code change), a rarity. In the architecture of Microcean frugality is built in as the norm, not as a feat: by design, every assembly declares its resource footprint in advance and asks for consent, and efficiency is checked as strictly as correctness.

3.How the system is arranged

The concept rests on seven architectural principles. Each of them, taken alone, rests on known science; the novelty lies in joining them into a single environment.

PrincipleContent
Micro-fragmentsThe basic unit of code is a small fragment (a function, an algorithm), not a large package. Large ready-made solutions are thereby preserved as "mature forms" — they can be used whole and copied; but the foundation remains the fragment.
Swarm of executorsThe task is carried out not by one universal intelligence but by many narrow specialized modules: neural and algorithmic. Coordination is handled by the top control level.
Evolutionary selectionAssembly variants compete with each other; the best combinations are preserved and developed — by the same principles that operate in evolutionary biology and genetic algorithms.
Peer-to-peer networkStoring and exchanging fragments are distributed among the participants of the network — without a central server. Search is built on the proven DHT protocols, state synchronization on CRDT.
Trust without blockchainThe reputation of participants is confirmed by executable tests and lightweight consensus algorithms (PBFT, Raft) — without energy-hungry computation.
Two modes of operationThe open ocean — a global network of maximum power; a lagoon — a closed circuit of a company or a state. One protocol, different access policies.
Resource responsibilityA principle in sketch status: an assembly, by design, declares its resource footprint in advance (download volume, storage, loads) and asks for consent. The fragment's metadata include a measured performance profile; efficiency tests are equal in standing to correctness tests, and selection is meant to cut off wasteful combinations. Frugality is the norm, not a feat.

4.The evidence base

Every key claim of the concept is backed by computations that anyone can repeat on their own — the source codes of the experiments are open. Key results:

  • Enumeration is impossible, but the task is solvable. The number of assembly combinations in the model of the publication exceeds 10101 — more than the atoms in the Universe. An evolutionary algorithm finds a correct assembly in roughly 20 thousand trials, without resorting to enumeration (30 successful outcomes out of 30 runs).
  • The network scales. Finding the needed fragment in a network of a billion nodes takes on average 5–6 steps; propagating an update to one hundred thousand nodes — about 11 exchange cycles. Network size still affects speed, but slowly: the growth in the number of steps is close to logarithmic.
  • "Zero excess" works. A size penalty shrinks the final assembly by a factor of 2.5 without loss of correctness.
  • The limits are named honestly. Absolute minimality of a program is mathematically uncomputable; a universal optimizer does not exist (the No Free Lunch theorem); parallelism is limited by Amdahl's law. The system is positioned as a strong solution for the class of tasks with structure, not as a universal remedy.

5.Security

The distributed architecture is conceived so as to remove the main causes of today's information-security incidents:

  1. No monoculture. The compromise of one fragment does not destroy the system: word of the threat spreads through the network, and participants adapt.
  2. No single point of failure. Like a torrent network, the Ocean keeps working even if part of the nodes goes down.
  3. Verification instead of trust. Every fragment is accompanied by executable tests; reputation is earned by a verifiable history.

6.Lagoons: closed circuits

The global reach of the system does not cancel the right to privacy. An organization can deploy a lagoon — a closed copy of the environment with its own fragments and its own history, designed so that nothing leaks outward. A lagoon is self-sufficient, yet without an inflow from outside its capabilities are modest — just as a local network yields to the global one in variety. Selective exchange is also possible — under the owner's rules.

A separate scenario is copying someone else's open ecosystem wholesale: if a public project (say, a store with open code) suits the organization, it can be moved inside its own circuit and refined for its tasks.

8.Philosophy and legal standing

Microcean describes not a single tool but a change of paradigm: the transition from "extracting" software to living in a shared environment. The concept is published openly and declared common heritage of all: it was recorded internally on 2026-08-11 and publicly disclosed on 2026-10-22 in a public GitHub repository — the commit history is verifiable evidence of the date, and the disclosed elements form publicly accessible prior art, an obstacle to future patents on what is disclosed. CC0 1.0 Universal is declared as the licensing intent; non-waivable authorship under RF Civil Code Art. 1265/1267 is retained by the author, while additional triple timestamping (DOI/OTS/notary) is in preparation. The author publishes the idea and runs no infrastructure whatsoever: he is not an operator of the system, not a beneficiary and not a founder of any legal entity. Openness here matters more than monopoly: the idea remains free for all.

Every great step of technology — from writing to the internet — at first raised questions and later became a common support of humankind. Microcean likewise proposes changes, and so people and organizations will naturally have questions: how to preserve what was invested, how to protect one's own, how to live by new rules. These questions are fair, and the answers are provided for in the architecture — for now in the status of design principles: closed circuits — lagoons — are meant to give organizations confidentiality; open mechanisms — transparency and verifiability; common heritage of all — an open rather than monopolistic arrangement, under which the author does not claim to restrict others.

We invite all participants of the digital world — individual developers, companies, states — not into a fight against the old but into a realization of the new: to assess, discuss and criticize the concept. Benefit here does not compete with security and does not cancel anyone's interests: it arises from the fact that every participant gains access to the accumulated experience of all humankind while retaining the full right to their own space.

It is important to emphasize: this publication proposes a paradigm — a way of thinking and a direction of development, not a program of action against anything or anyone. The only call contained here is a call to creation: to study, discuss and develop it by lawful means for the benefit of all. Participation is voluntary; everyone is free to accept the idea wholly, in part or not at all — and any of these choices is respected.
