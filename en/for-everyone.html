MICROCEAN
One ocean from a billion drops

What Microcean is

Picture one space in which all the software of the world lives. Today programs are scattered in bits: some in app stores, some on developers' websites, some in huge collections of ready-made parts. In Microcean all of this joins into one whole, the way streams join into a river — and the author calls it the Ocean of Code.

Gathering a program yourself is no longer needed — just as you don't need to assemble a car from spare parts to drive to the store. It is enough to say, in ordinary words, what you want:

  • a schoolchild: "I want a game about a rocket and the stars";
  • an adult: "I need a reminder program for Mom that tells me if she missed her medicine";
  • a business owner: "assemble my workshop's website with a storefront and card payments";
  • a specialist: "assemble warehouse bookkeeping with inventory and reports".

The Ocean itself finds suitable parts all over the world, joins them, tests them and hands over a finished program. From millions of drops one ocean is made — not an archipelago of islands, but continuous water.

From millions of small drops one ocean is made. So it is with Microcean: from billions of tiny code fragments an environment is made, able to assemble a wide class of tasks — only within the limits that the publication itself names honestly.

What's not all right today

Right now programs are assembled from large ready-made blocks — libraries. Imagine that to fix a faucet you have to buy the entire bathroom set: the faucet you need takes up one percent of the purchase, and the rest must be stored somewhere. That is exactly how today's development works: to use a few useful functions, a project pulls in a library whole. Modern build tools can trim unused code, yet tens and hundreds of thousands of needless lines still often remain in the finished program.

This is inconvenient and dangerous. When millions of programs depend on the same library, an error in it becomes a worldwide problem. It has already happened:

  • 2016: one developer deleted a tiny helper package of 11 lines — and thousands of projects worldwide stopped building.
  • 2020: attackers planted a backdoor in an update program: about eighteen thousand organizations downloaded it, and hundreds were actually compromised.
  • 2021–2024: dangerous vulnerabilities were discovered in libraries used by hundreds of millions of devices.

And there are two more troubles that are rarely spoken of. The first — wastefulness without asking. Try building a popular browser from source code: without your noticing, many gigabytes of auxiliary components will land on your computer. No one asks whether you are ready for that; and the report of what was downloaded and why is hidden in service logs that the ordinary user, as a rule, never looks into. The second — reluctance to optimize. Programs are made correct, but almost never frugal: tests for speed and a modest appetite for resources are a great rarity; at individual giants like Firefox and Chromium such checks run on almost every code change, while for most others they appear when everything already works badly. In Microcean the answer is built into the architecture itself: by design, before installation you will be told the price honestly — how much traffic and storage will be needed — and frugal solutions will be selected automatically, the way correct ones are. Torrent-for-code at the micro-level: P2P, take exactly what you need, no excess traffic. Library vs Internet — the same expressive difference: before you went to the library, carried books; now you type a query — and everything is there.

How it works

The metaphor of water explains the behavior of the system better than anything.

Water takes the shape of any vessel. Pour it into a cup — it becomes the shape of the cup; into a bucket — the shape of the bucket. Programs in the Ocean adjust to your task exactly the same way: it is not you who bends to the program, the program is assembled for you.

Water closes up behind an object. Take a spoon out of a glass — the water closes itself. If an error or a malicious piece is found in a program, the Ocean "closes the breach": word of it spreads through the network like a sea wave, and participants update their copies before they can be harmed.

A river does not run dry because a bucket was scooped. The Ocean has no single center that can be switched off. Just as torrents work without a main server, so here: data is scattered among millions of participants. No rigid gradation: you can assemble from drops, take a school of fish, or grab a whole island — there are practically no restrictions.

How we know this is possible

The idea has been put through a serious check rather than left a fantasy. Scientists and engineers did the following:

  1. Looked for anything similar in the world. More than a hundred papers and projects were studied. No exact twin was found: there are similar individual details, but nobody has yet built a whole system like this.
  2. Recounted the mathematics. The number of possible combinations when assembling a program in the model of the publication exceeds the number of atoms in the Universe — so simple enumeration cannot solve the task. But the evolutionary method (the same principle by which nature selects the fittest) finds the right solution in roughly twenty thousand attempts. Confirmed by computer experiments: thirty runs — thirty successful outcomes.
  3. Checked the network. Even if the participants number a billion, finding the needed fragment will take five or six steps: network size affects speed weakly — the growth is roughly logarithmic, slow.
  4. Everything can be checked yourself. The source code of all computations is open: anyone with a computer can run it and get the same numbers. Honesty here is not a promise but a property of the publication.

We state the limits honestly too: there are no miracles, and some things are mathematically impossible in any system in the world. The full scientific analysis is open to everyone — see the links at the bottom of the page.

Why this is safer than today's internet

  • There is no main door to be broken into. The network is distributed among millions of participants — it cannot be destroyed as a whole.
  • An error localizes itself. A damaged fragment is replaced by a healthy one, and word of an attack spreads through the network faster than the attack itself.
  • Trust is earned by deeds. Every code fragment passes automatic trials. A participant's reputation is a history of passed checks, not promises.

But what about secrecy? Lagoons

Not everything should be shared. A company may want its internal programs never to leave its walls; a state — that army code live only in a closed network. For that the system has lagoons: quiet, enclosed patches of the Ocean with their own code and their own history, designed so that nothing leaks outward from them.

A lagoon is fully self-sufficient, but without an inflow of new knowledge from outside it is poorer than the global environment — roughly as a home library is poorer than a city one. Exchange with the big Ocean is possible, but only under the lagoon owner's rules. And there is a simpler way: take someone else's open ecosystem whole — the way a stream is transplanted into one's own pond — and refine it for oneself. Library vs Internet — the same expressive difference.
